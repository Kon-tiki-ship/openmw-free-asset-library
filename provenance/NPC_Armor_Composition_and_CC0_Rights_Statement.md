# Generic Legacy Assets, NPC Armor Composition and CC0 Rights Statement

## 1. Purpose and Scope

This statement explains how certain legacy 3D assets used in the project, together with NPC armor combinations created from those assets, are treated for copyright and licensing purposes. The rights claim made here is not that any third-party 3D file of uncertain provenance was created by me, that the individual copyrights in such files have transferred to me, or that uncertainty regarding provenance automatically places those assets in the public domain or otherwise makes them free to use. The narrower claim is that the project contains an independently created layer of selection, coordination, and arrangement through which particular assets were selected, combined, assigned to NPC bodies, matched with one another, renamed, configured, and defined within a structured data system, and that any copyright or related rights that I may hold in those original contributions are dedicated to the public under CC0 1.0 Universal. Nothing in this statement should be interpreted as asserting that I have acquired, extinguished, or relicensed any independent rights that unidentified third parties may hold in the underlying legacy materials.

## 2. Nature of the Legacy Assets Examined

The provenance review conducted for the relevant assets indicates that they are not original Bethesda/Morrowind game assets, have not been identified as part of the commercial *Ultima IX: Ascension* asset set, cannot on the basis of presently available records be reliably attributed to the original *Ultima IX* development team, and appear instead to have emerged or circulated through later modding projects. In some cases, the original creator can no longer be identified, while a substantial portion of the assets represents common object categories such as swords, gloves, boots, cuirasses, pauldrons, and historical or fantasy armor components. Neither uncertain authorship nor “orphan” status is treated here as evidence that such material has entered the public domain or may automatically be used without restriction. The project's legal position instead depends upon distinguishing any protectable original expression that may exist within the underlying asset from generic, historical, functional, stock, or commonplace elements, as well as from the separately created compositional layer introduced by the project.

## 3. Generic, Stock, Historical, and Functional Elements

Under the U.S. Copyright Act, copyright protection begins with original expression. **17 U.S.C. §102(a)** limits copyright protection to “original works of authorship,” while §102(b) excludes ideas, methods, systems, processes, concepts, and similar subject matter from copyright protection. In *Satava v. Lowry*, 323 F.3d 805 (9th Cir. 2003), the Ninth Circuit likewise recognized that “standard, stock, or common” elements associated with a particular subject may not be monopolized by a single artist through copyright, while independently original choices of form, color, ornamentation, and arrangement may remain protectable. Accordingly, object categories such as an ordinary straight sword, historical Roman-style armor, generic knight armor, simple fur clothing, leather gloves, ordinary boots, or a standard cuirass, together with their basic historical or functional forms, are not themselves exclusive creative expression. This does not mean, however, that a particular digital model as a whole is necessarily unprotectable. Original sculptural form, stylization, ornamentation, surface design, creative motifs, texture work, or other distinctive artistic choices may separately constitute protectable expression.

## 4. Being a Digital 3D Model Is Not by Itself Sufficient for Copyright Protection

The fact that an asset exists as a `.nif`, `.obj`, `.dae`, or comparable digital 3D file does not by itself determine the existence or scope of copyright protection. In this respect, *Meshwerks, Inc. v. Toyota Motor Sales U.S.A., Inc.*, 528 F.3d 1258 (10th Cir. 2008), illustrates that extensive technical effort and sophisticated digital modeling are not independently sufficient to establish copyrightable authorship. The case concerned digital wireframe models created to reproduce the appearance of Toyota vehicles as faithfully as possible, and although the court recognized that digital modeling tools are fully capable of producing copyrightable works, it found insufficient independent original expression in the particular models at issue. The decision should not be read as establishing that generic fantasy armor models are automatically uncopyrightable. Its narrower and more relevant proposition is that vertex count, technical skill, file complexity, and labor invested in production are legally distinct from the question of copyrightable original expression.

## 5. Distinguishing Individual Assets from NPC and Armor-Set Compositions

Within the project, individual assets such as helmets, cuirasses, left and right pauldrons, gloves, greaves, boots, body meshes, and weapons are not treated merely as isolated files. Additional choices are made concerning which parts are used together, which NPC or body receives them, which equipment slots they occupy, how individual pieces are matched visually and functionally, how the resulting character is named, and how that character is represented through records, metadata, and related game-data relationships. This process results in an **NPC configuration / armor-set composition / structured game-data arrangement** that is conceptually distinct from any individual underlying file such as `glove.nif` or `cuirass.nif`. Whether that new layer qualifies for copyright protection depends not merely on its novelty or the amount of labor invested in creating it, but on whether the relevant choices of selection, coordination, and arrangement satisfy the applicable minimum threshold of originality.

## 6. 17 U.S.C. §101 and the Concept of a Compilation

**17 U.S.C. §101** defines a “compilation” as a work formed by the collection and assembling of preexisting materials or data that are “selected, coordinated, or arranged” in such a way that the resulting work as a whole constitutes an original work of authorship. The underlying components of a compilation therefore need not all be owned by the person who creates the compilation. The relevant legal question is whether sufficient independent originality exists in the selection, coordination, or arrangement of the preexisting material. The rights claim made by this project is framed accordingly: it is not a claim to ownership of the individual mesh or texture files themselves, but, to the extent the applicable originality threshold is met, a claim limited to the independently created selection, coordination, and arrangement of those materials.

## 7. Feist v. Rural and the Principle of Original Selection and Arrangement

In *Feist Publications, Inc. v. Rural Telephone Service Co.*, 499 U.S. 340 (1991), the U.S. Supreme Court recognized that even unprotected facts or materials may form part of a protectable compilation where their selection, coordination, or arrangement is sufficiently original, while making clear that the resulting protection extends to the original selection and arrangement rather than to the underlying material itself. The Court also rejected the “sweat of the brow” doctrine, establishing that the amount of labor invested in producing a work does not by itself create copyright protection and that at least a minimal degree of creativity is required. Accordingly, the originality claim in this project does not rest on mechanical activities such as collecting files, converting formats, or creating alphabetical lists. It rests instead on evaluating different assets, deliberately selecting particular components, creating new armor sets, associating those sets with particular NPC identities and in-game roles, establishing naming choices, and arranging those elements within meaningful metadata and data relationships.

## 8. 17 U.S.C. §103 and the Limits of Rights in Preexisting Material

**17 U.S.C. §103(b)** provides that copyright in a compilation or derivative work extends only to the material contributed by the author of the new work and does not create exclusive rights in the preexisting material itself. The position of this project is therefore not that “the rights of the unidentified creator of this glove, armor model, or other file now belong to me.” Rather, the claim is that, where the relevant copyrightability threshold is satisfied, an independent original contribution may exist in the selection of particular components, the way those components are coordinated on a particular character, the resulting armor-set composition, the NPC configuration, creative naming, metadata selection, and the arrangement of particular data content. Even where such protection exists, it does not confer ownership over the underlying assets, ideas, generic forms, or the independent right of others to use the same or similar underlying material.

## 9. Data Structures, Metadata, and System Elements

When project records, metadata relationships, classifications, and data arrangements are considered, a distinction must be maintained between copyrightable expression and unprotectable ideas, methods, and systems. This statement does not assert exclusive copyright ownership over a database schema, technical method, relationship logic, or system functionality as such. The rights claim is limited, where sufficient originality exists, to the creative selection and arrangement of database content and project metadata, character-specific naming choices, the association of particular assets with particular records, and the concrete project expression produced by those choices. In this way, the **creative selection and arrangement of database content and project metadata** is distinguished from underlying methods, systems, and functional logic that copyright does not protect.

## 10. Underlying Legacy Assets and Original Project Contributions

The project distinguishes between two separate layers of rights. The first consists of individual legacy meshes, textures, and other preexisting assets. If known or unknown third parties hold rights in those files, this statement does not claim that such rights have been extinguished, transferred to me, or become subject to relicensing by me. The second layer consists of original project contributions created by me, including armor-set selection, NPC composition, equipment coordination, body and equipment assignments, character configurations, creative naming, metadata content, and related record relationships. To the extent that copyright or related rights arise in this second layer, the rights claim is limited exclusively to my own independently created authorship and does not create new ownership rights in the underlying legacy materials.

## 11. Scope of the CC0 1.0 Universal Dedication

The **CC0 1.0 Universal** dedication used in this project applies only to copyright and related rights that I own or control. Creative Commons' CC0 framework is likewise designed to allow a rights holder to waive or dedicate their own rights to the public to the fullest extent legally possible; it does not permit one person to extinguish rights held by unrelated third parties. CC0 is therefore used here in the following sense: **To the extent that copyright or related rights subsist in my independently created NPC armor compositions, selection, coordination, arrangement, naming, metadata content, and related original project contributions, I dedicate those rights to the public under CC0 1.0 Universal to the fullest extent legally possible.** This dedication does not mean that any unidentified third-party rights in underlying assets of unresolved provenance are claimed, transferred, waived, or extinguished by me.

## 12. Conclusion

The legal position of this project rests on three principal distinctions. First, generic, historical, functional, stock, or commonplace armor and weapon elements are legally distinct from any independently original artistic expression that may exist within a particular digital model. Second, an individual legacy asset is legally distinct from a new layer created through the original selection, coordination, and arrangement of multiple assets into particular NPCs and armor sets; where the applicable originality threshold is satisfied, that layer may constitute a limited compilation or another form of copyrightable authorship. Third, the rights dedicated to the public under CC0 are not rights of ownership in unidentified third-party assets, but only those rights that I legally hold in my own original authorship. The project's position may therefore be summarized as follows: **The project does not claim authorship or ownership of unidentified legacy components merely because they are incorporated into the project. Where individual components consist principally of generic, historical, functional, stock, or otherwise unprotectable elements, no exclusive ownership over those underlying concepts or standard forms is asserted. The project does, however, claim authorship, to the extent protectable under applicable law, in its independently created selection, coordination, arrangement, NPC configurations, armor-set compositions, creative naming, metadata content, and data relationships. To the extent that copyright or related rights subsist in those original contributions, those rights are dedicated to the public under CC0 1.0 Universal. No representation is made that CC0 extinguishes, transfers, or licenses any independently existing rights of unidentified third parties in underlying legacy materials.** Put differently, uncertainty concerning the history of individual components does not automatically negate the existence of a newly created layer of selection and composition, but rights in that new layer likewise do not transfer to me any independently existing rights in the underlying components.
